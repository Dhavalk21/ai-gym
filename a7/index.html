Zone 4, Measure quality

Evals

How to know whether your AI feature is any good: look at the data, pick the right graders, and build a golden set that guards every change. Then build an eval and test the judge.

1Error analysis firstBefore you measure anything, look at what your AI actually does.8 min

The idea

An eval is a repeatable test of your AI feature's quality. Most teams start in the wrong place: they pick a generic score, or ask a model to rate everything from 1 to 10. Those numbers rarely match what users experience.

Practitioners such as Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar start instead with error analysis: read real outputs, note what goes wrong, and let the failures you find decide what to measure.

The framework

Error analysis in four steps:

1. Collect→2. Note→3. Group→4. Count
  1. Collect 50 to 100 real interactions (or realistic ones before launch).
  2. Note what's wrong in each, in your own words. Don't start from a list of categories.
  3. Group the notes into a few failure types: wrong facts, invented actions, ignored conditions, unhelpful refusals.
  4. Count them. The biggest groups are where your evals, and your fixes, should start.

This is product work, not just engineering work. The PM often knows best what "wrong" means for users.

Real example

Rechat, a real-estate software company, built an AI assistant called Lucy. Its early prototype was unreliable. In talks and writing with Hamel Husain, Rechat's CTO Emil Sedgh described how the team got it to production: by logging real interactions, reviewing them, and building evals specific to their domain rather than relying on generic benchmarks.

Source: Hamel Husain, "Your AI Product Needs Evals", March 2024, and the AI Engineer talk with Emil Sedgh.

Where it breaks

  • Generic metrics mislead. Text-similarity scores and 1-to-10 ratings rarely track what makes your product good or bad.
  • Only reading the happy path hides failures. Sample at random, and add the awkward cases on purpose.
  • Delegating the looking to a tool before you've done it yourself means you won't know what the tool is missing.

2Code checks, AI judges and peopleThree kinds of grader. Use the cheapest one that can do the job.9 min

The idea

Once you know your failure types, each needs a grader:

GraderBest forWatch out for
Code checksExact things: valid JSON, required fields, numbers, banned words, lengthCan't judge meaning or tone
AI judge (LLM-as-judge)Fuzzy things at scale: grounded in the source? answered the question? right tone?Biases; must be checked against people
PeopleThe gold standard; labelling the test set; judging new failure typesSlow and costly, so use them to calibrate, not for everything

The framework

Writing a judge you can trust:

  1. One criterion per judge, as a yes/no question: "Does the answer claim an action the assistant can't take?"
  2. Give it what it needs: the source document or a reference answer for factual checks.
  3. Pass or fail, not 1 to 10, plus a one-sentence reason you can read.
  4. Test the judge against your own labels: how many of your fails does it catch, and how many of your passes does it keep?

Real example

In a 2023 study, researchers behind Chatbot Arena tested whether a strong model could judge chatbot answers. GPT-4 as judge agreed with human preferences over 80% of the time, about the same as humans agree with each other. They also documented biases: judges favoured the first answer shown (position bias), longer answers (verbosity bias) and their own outputs (self-enhancement bias).

Source: Zheng et al., "Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena", NeurIPS 2023.

Where it breaks

  • High overall agreement can hide misses. If 95% of answers pass, a judge that passes everything scores 95% "accuracy" and catches nothing. Check fails separately.
  • Judges drift when you change their model or prompt. Re-check them against labels.
  • A judge isn't a fix. It tells you something is wrong; the fix is in the prompt, the retrieval or the product.

3Golden sets and eval-driven developmentTurn your evals into a safety net that runs on every change.9 min

The idea

A golden set is a fixed collection of inputs, with expected results or pass rules, that represents what your feature must handle: common cases, awkward cases and every serious failure you've seen. Every change to the prompt, model or retrieval runs against it before it ships.

That's eval-driven development: the same idea as automated tests for code, applied to AI behaviour.

The framework

The improvement loop:

Look at failures→Add them to the golden set→Fix→Run the set→Ship if it passes
  • Size: start with 20 to 50 cases; grow it from real failures.
  • A launch bar: agree the pass rate needed before launch, per failure type. Some types (invented refunds) need zero tolerance.
  • Offline and online: golden sets catch regressions before release; production monitoring (A8) catches what the set never imagined.

Real example

In December 2024 and January 2025, Apple Intelligence's notification summaries produced false news alerts, including one that wrongly said a murder suspect had shot himself, shown under the BBC's name. After complaints from the BBC and others, Apple paused summaries for news and entertainment apps in its iOS 18.3 betas, and added italics and a warning that summaries may contain errors.

Source: reporting by TechCrunch and CNN, 16 and 17 January 2025.

Where it breaks

  • A set that never grows goes stale. Add every new failure you find in production.
  • Overfitting: tuning prompts to pass the set can hurt real users. Keep a hidden part of the set you don't tune against.
  • Evals need owners. If nobody looks at results or adds cases, the safety net quietly disappears.

Cases

One team that got a shaky AI assistant to production by looking at its data, and one launch that shipped false headlines to millions of phones.

Got it right

Rechat's Lucy

Rechat built Lucy, an AI assistant for real-estate agents. Its first prototype, built on GPT-3.5, worked in demos but was unreliable. Working with Hamel Husain, the team logged real interactions, reviewed them systematically and built evals specific to their domain, from simple code assertions to model-graded checks, which let them improve Lucy step by step towards production (Hamel Husain, "Your AI Product Needs Evals", 2024; AI Engineer talk with Rechat's CTO Emil Sedgh).

Look at your data, write domain-specific tests, and let them steer every change.

Got it wrong

Apple's notification summaries

Apple Intelligence began summarising iPhone notifications in iOS 18.2. In December 2024 a summary falsely told users, under the BBC's name, that the suspect in a high-profile murder case had shot himself; other false summaries followed. After complaints from the BBC and press groups, Apple paused summaries for news and entertainment apps in January 2025, and added italics and a warning that summaries may be wrong (TechCrunch, 16 January 2025).

For a feature that rewrites facts at scale, the eval must test faithfulness to the source before launch, and keep testing after.

Show a worked example

Feature: Ledgerly's support assistant. Top failures (from reading 80 conversations): wrong facts (11), invented actions like refunds (4), ignored conditions such as reverse charge for consumers (6), unhelpful refusals (5).

Code checks: no answer contains "I've refunded", "I've changed" or "I've cancelled"; every answer cites a help article. Judges: one each for "supported by the cited article?", "conditions respected?" and "refused when the article had the answer?", each validated against 60 human labels.

Golden set: 50 real questions, including German, French and two-topic ones. Launch bar: zero invented actions, at least 90% supported answers, refusals under 5%. Runs: on every prompt or model change, and nightly on a fresh sample.

Key takeaways

  1. Start with error analysis. Read real outputs, note what goes wrong, group and count: your failures decide what to measure.
  2. Use the cheapest grader that works. Code for exact checks, AI judges for fuzzy ones, people to label and calibrate.
  3. Judges need judging. One yes/no criterion each, the source to check against, and a measured catch rate before you trust them.
  4. Make evals the safety net. A golden set that grows from real failures, a launch bar, and a run on every change.
