Zone 1, Understand the machine

Where AI fits

Which problems deserve AI, what role people keep, and what stays yours when every competitor has the same models. Then score your own ideas.

The situation

Ledgerly's board wants "an AI strategy" by the next meeting. The team has a long list of ideas, from a pricing-page chatbot to auto-sent reminders. You get to choose what goes forward.

"Everyone's adding AI. Which of these actually helps our users, and which ones are we doing just because we can?"

Why predict first? Trying a question before the lesson makes the lesson stick better, even when the first guess is wrong. Rating your confidence trains you to notice what you don't know yet.

Lessons

Three short lessons. Each runs the same way: the idea, the framework, a real example, where it breaks, and a quick drill.

1Automate or augment?Decide the person's role before you pick a model.8 min

The idea

Every AI feature makes a choice about people. Automation means the AI does the task and nobody checks each result. Augmentation means the AI helps a person do the task: it suggests, drafts or finds, and the person decides.

Google's People + AI Guidebook frames it as a question to research with users: automate tasks people find hard, boring or unpleasant; augment tasks people enjoy, feel responsible for, or where the stakes are high.

Augmentation is where much of the proven value sits. In a controlled experiment by GitHub and Microsoft researchers, developers with GitHub Copilot finished a coding task 55.8% faster than those without it (Peng et al., 2023).

The framework

The autonomy ladder. Start low, and climb only with evidence:

1. SuggestsThe person chooses from options.
2. DraftsThe person edits and sends.
3. Acts on OKAI does it after the person approves.
4. Acts, auditedAI does it; people check samples.
5. Acts aloneNo routine human check.

Pick the rung from two things: what a mistake costs, and how easily it can be undone. Move up when the data shows people stopped correcting the AI.

Real example

In a 2023 experiment with 758 Boston Consulting Group consultants, those using GPT-4 on tasks inside its abilities completed 12.2% more tasks, 25.1% faster, with more than 40% higher quality. But on a task chosen to sit just outside its abilities, consultants using AI were 19 percentage points less likely to get the right answer than those without it.

The researchers call this the jagged frontier: tasks that look equally hard can fall on either side of what AI does well.

Source: Dell'Acqua, Mollick and others, "Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier", Harvard Business School working paper 24-013, 2023.

Where it breaks

  • Rubber-stamping: a "human in the loop" who approves everything without reading is automation with extra steps. Make checking easy, and watch the approval rate.
  • The frontier moves, unevenly: a task that failed last year may work now, and a similar-looking task may still fail. Test each one.
  • Users may not want it: the PAIR Guidebook warns that some people simply don't want a task automated. Ask them.

2The use-case canvasScore ideas before you build them, and ask if a rule would do.9 min

The idea

Good AI use cases sit where a real, frequent user problem meets something AI is unusually good at: messy language, images, summaries, drafts and personalisation. Poor ones need exact answers, predictable behaviour, or full transparency, which ordinary code gives you for free.

Most AI ideas fail long before the model matters: they solve a problem nobody has, or they never fit into how people actually work.

The framework

The canvas: score each idea from 1 to 5 on four questions.

ValueHow painful and how frequent is the problem?
FitIs it messy language or judgement, or would a rule do?
DataDo we have the context the model needs?
Cost of errorHow bad is a mistake, and can it be undone?

Rank by value × fit × data. Then use the cost of error to pick the rung on the autonomy ladder, not to kill a good idea outright. Any idea where "a rule would do" scores 1 on fit: build the rule.

Real example

In July 2025, a report from MIT's Project NANDA, "The GenAI Divide", said 95% of the organisations it studied saw no measurable return from generative AI, and blamed integration and learning gaps more than the models. Treat the number with care: critics, including Wharton professor Kevin Werbach, questioned how transparent its method and sample were.

The useful lesson survives either way: picking the use case and fitting it into real work decide more than the model does.

Where it breaks

  • Scores are guesses. Check "fit" by trying the idea on 20 real examples in a playground before anyone builds anything.
  • AI theatre: features that demo well but nobody uses. If you can't name the user and the problem, you're building theatre.
  • The canvas favours the obvious. Leave room for one bolder bet, and test it cheaply.

3Build, buy or partner, and real moatsWhat stays yours when every competitor has the same models?9 min

The idea

You have three paths. Buy: use AI that ships inside tools you already pay for. Partner: build your feature on a model provider's API, which is what most product teams do. Build: train or host your own models, which only pays off with unusual data, scale or privacy needs.

Whichever you choose, the models keep getting better and cheaper for everyone, including your competitors. So "we use the best model" is never an advantage for long.

The framework

The moat check: ask what stays yours when the model improves for everyone.

  1. Your data: context nobody else has, such as each client's payment history.
  2. Your workflow: AI placed where the work already happens, so it's easier than switching tools.
  3. Your feedback loop: corrections that make the feature better the more it's used.
  4. Your trust and distribution: users who already rely on you with their money or data.

A feature with none of these is a thin wrapper, and the next model release can copy it.

Real example

Jasper, an AI writing tool for marketers, raised $125 million at a $1.5 billion valuation in October 2022, largely on top of OpenAI's models. Weeks later, ChatGPT launched for free. In 2023 Jasper laid off staff, cut its revenue forecast by at least 30% and lowered its internal valuation by about 20%, according to reporting by The Information. It then refocused on enterprise marketing teams.

Where it breaks

  • "Data moat" is often overrated. Data only protects you if it's unique and makes answers measurably better.
  • Platform risk: the provider you build on can ship your feature. Build where you own the workflow.
  • Lock-in: keep the model swappable behind your own interface, so a better or cheaper model is a change, not a rebuild.

Cases

One company that used AI to make people faster, and one that pushed automation too far and had to walk it back.

Got it right

GitHub Copilot

Before launching Copilot widely, GitHub and Microsoft researchers ran a controlled experiment in May and June 2022 with 95 freelance developers. Each was asked to build an HTTP server in JavaScript as fast as possible. Those with Copilot finished 55.8% faster than those without (Peng et al., "The Impact of AI on Developer Productivity", 2023). Copilot suggests; the developer decides what to keep.

Augment first, and measure the gain in a real task before you claim it.

Got it wrong

Klarna's customer service

In February 2024, Klarna said its OpenAI-powered assistant was doing the work of 700 full-time customer service agents, and the company had paused hiring. In May 2025, CEO Sebastian Siemiatkowski told Bloomberg that cost had been "too predominant" a factor and the result was lower quality, and Klarna started hiring human agents again so customers could always reach a person.

Optimising only for cost hides the cost of errors. Keep a human path, and measure quality, not just volume.

Show a worked example

Idea: draft late-payment reminders in Ledgerly. Problem: freelancers put off chasing clients because it's awkward, so they get paid later.

Canvas: value 5 (frequent and painful), fit 4 (tone and wording are language work), data 4 (Ledgerly has the invoice and payment history), cost of error 3 (it goes to a real client). Score 80.

Mode and rung: augment, starting at rung 2: AI drafts in the user's tone, the user edits and sends. Watch the edit rate; offer auto-send for gentle first reminders once edits drop near zero.

Moat: each client's payment history inside Ledgerly lets the reminder pick the right moment and tone. A generic chatbot can't do that.

Key takeaways

  1. Decide the person's role before the model. Automate where mistakes are cheap and reversible; augment where judgement or trust matters, and climb with evidence.
  2. Score ideas on value, fit, data and cost of error. If a rule would do, build the rule.
  3. The model isn't your moat. Your data, your workflow, your feedback loop and your users' trust are.
  4. Test the riskiest assumption cheaply first. Most AI projects stall on fit and integration, not on the model.
