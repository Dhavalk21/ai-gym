1The EU AI Act on one pageFour tiers, two roles and a handful of dates. Enough to spot what applies to your product.10 min

The idea

The EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) is the first broad law on AI. It regulates by risk: the more an AI system can harm people's safety or rights, the more duties apply. It covers AI systems placed on the EU market or used in the EU, wherever the company is based.

It also distinguishes roles. The provider develops the system and puts it on the market; the deployer uses it in its own work. A company that builds on a model API and ships a feature is usually a provider of that feature; a company that buys a tool for HR is a deployer.

This station is practical orientation for product managers, not legal advice. Check your own case with your legal or privacy team.

The framework

TierExamplesApplies from
Prohibited (Art. 5)Social scoring; manipulation that causes harm; emotion recognition at work or school; untargeted scraping of faces2 Feb 2025; two new bans (non-consensual intimate imagery, child sexual abuse material) from 2 Dec 2026
High-risk (Annex III)Hiring and worker management, education, credit scoring, life and health insurance pricing, biometrics, critical infrastructure, law enforcement, migration, justice2 Dec 2027 (AI in regulated products, Annex I: 2 Aug 2028)
Transparency (Art. 50)Chatbots must say they're AI; deepfakes must be labelled; generative AI output must be marked as AI-made2 Aug 2026 (marking for systems already on the market: 2 Dec 2026)
Minimal riskSpam filters, invoice OCR, expense categorisationNo new AI Act duties

Also in force: AI literacy (Art. 4) since February 2025, now worded as taking measures to support staff's AI literacy, and duties for general-purpose AI model providers since August 2025. Fines reach €35 million or 7% of worldwide turnover for prohibited practices, and €15 million or 3% for most other breaches.

Real example

The timeline has already moved once. In November 2025 the European Commission proposed a "Digital Omnibus" to simplify the Act. Parliament endorsed it on 16 June 2026, the Council adopted it on 29 June, and it entered into force on 27 July 2026 as Regulation (EU) 2026/1744. It postponed high-risk duties from August 2026 to December 2027, softened the AI literacy duty, and added two new bans, while the transparency duties kept their August 2026 date.

Sources: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 on EUR-Lex; Bird & Bird and Gibson Dunn briefings on the Omnibus agreement (May 2026); Council adoption, 29 June 2026.

Where it breaks

  • "We're not an AI company" doesn't matter. A feature that screens CVs or scores credit is high-risk wherever it lives.
  • The delay isn't a pause. Bans and transparency duties already apply, and high-risk work (data, documentation, oversight) takes longer than a year to do well.
  • Dates and details keep changing through guidelines and standards. Re-check before a launch, not once a year.

2GDPR meets AIIf personal data goes in or comes out, GDPR has applied all along.9 min

The idea

The AI Act sits alongside the GDPR. Most AI features touch personal data somewhere: in prompts, in retrieved documents, in logs and traces (A8), or in training data. For those, GDPR questions come first, and they're the ones that have been enforced for years.

The framework

Six GDPR questions for any AI feature:

  1. Lawful basis and purpose: why may you use this data for this AI purpose? Using customer data to train models is a new purpose and needs its own basis.
  2. Minimisation: does the model need names, emails or IDs? Strip or mask what it doesn't.
  3. Where does it go? Which AI vendor, which region, is there a data processing agreement, and does the vendor train on your data?
  4. Transparency: does your privacy notice explain the AI processing in plain words?
  5. Automated decisions (Art. 22): if the AI alone makes decisions with legal or similarly significant effects, people have rights to human intervention and to contest.
  6. Impact assessment (Art. 35): a DPIA is needed when processing is likely to be high risk, which new AI uses on personal data often are.

Real example

In December 2024, Italy's data protection authority fined OpenAI €15 million over ChatGPT, citing the legal basis for training on personal data, transparency, missing age checks and an unreported data breach. In March 2026 the Court of Rome annulled the decision. The reasoning published later turned on jurisdiction: Ireland's regulator had become the lead authority for OpenAI under the GDPR's one-stop-shop, so the court did not rule that the practices themselves were lawful.

Sources: Garante press release, 20 December 2024; Reuters and Wilson Sonsini on the Rome ruling, March 2026.

Where it breaks

  • Traces and logs are personal data too. Agree retention and masking before you switch on observability.
  • "The vendor handles GDPR" isn't true. You stay the controller for your users' data.
  • A human who rubber-stamps AI decisions isn't meaningful human intervention. They need time, information and authority to disagree.

3Documentation that holds upResponsible AI is a set of habits, written down. Start small and keep it current.9 min

The idea

Regulators, enterprise customers and your own future team will ask the same questions: what AI do you use, how risky is it, how do you know it works, and who's accountable? Good documentation answers them, and it's mostly things you've already built in this gym.

The framework

A lightweight responsible-AI pack per feature:

DocumentWhat it holdsFrom
AI register entryFeature, purpose, model and vendor, data used, your role, risk tierThis station
Risk and impact notesWho could be harmed and how; DPIA if needed; fundamental rights impact assessment where requiredA2, this station
Evaluation evidenceGolden set, results, launch bar, judge checksA7
Guardrails and oversightControls, human approval points, monitoring and incident processA8, A9
Transparency noticeWhat users are told about the AI, in plain wordsThis station

Two frameworks help structure it: the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (Govern, Map, Measure, Manage) and ISO/IEC 42001, a certifiable AI management system standard.

Real example

NIST released version 1.0 of its AI Risk Management Framework in January 2023, with a free playbook of suggested actions for each function. It's voluntary and not EU law, but many teams use its four functions as the backbone of their AI governance: Govern (roles and policies), Map (context and risks), Measure (testing and metrics) and Manage (prioritise, respond, monitor).

Source: NIST, AI Risk Management Framework.

Where it breaks

  • A binder nobody updates is worse than none: it shows you knew and stopped looking. Tie updates to releases.
  • Copying a template without doing the thinking produces documents that fall apart at the first question.
  • Leaving it to legal misses the product facts. The PM knows what the feature does, with which data, for whom.

Cases

A company that retired an AI capability before any law required it, and a government whose risk algorithm wrongly accused thousands of families.

Got it right

Microsoft retires emotion inference

In June 2022, publishing version 2 of its Responsible AI Standard, Microsoft announced it would retire Azure Face capabilities that inferred emotional states and attributes such as gender and age, citing privacy concerns, the lack of scientific consensus on reading emotions from faces, and risks of stereotyping and discrimination. New customers lost access immediately; existing ones by June 2023. It also put facial recognition behind an application process (Microsoft blog, 21 June 2022). Less than three years later, in February 2025, the EU AI Act banned emotion recognition at work and in schools.

Run your own risk review before the law makes you, and be willing to retire a capability that can't be made safe.

Got it wrong

The Dutch childcare benefits scandal

For years, the Dutch tax authority used risk profiles, including a self-learning model, to flag childcare benefit claims for fraud checks, and used applicants' dual nationality as a factor. Tens of thousands of families were wrongly treated as fraudsters, many pushed into debt. The government resigned in January 2021 after a parliamentary report, and in December 2021 the Dutch data protection authority fined the tax authority €2.75 million for processing nationality in an unlawful and discriminatory way (Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens; IAPP).

An algorithm that decides people's access to essential services needs bias testing, explainable outcomes, real human review and a way to appeal. Today it would be high-risk under the AI Act.

Show a worked example

Feature: Ledgerly's AI help assistant, "Lea". Tier: transparency duty (it talks to people). Role: provider of the feature, built on a third-party model.

Duties: say clearly that Lea is AI at the start of every chat; AI literacy measures for the support team. GDPR: legitimate interest for support; names and emails masked in traces; 30-day retention; vendor DPA with EU hosting and no training on our data; DPIA done because it's new processing of customer messages.

Notice: "Hi, I'm Lea, Ledgerly's AI assistant. I answer from our help articles and can't change your account. A person is one click away." Evidence: register entry, golden set and launch bar (A7), guardrail plan (A8). Owner: PM and privacy lead, reviewed before each major release and when the law's guidance changes.

Key takeaways

  1. Check in order: banned practice? Annex III high-risk area? Transparency duty? Otherwise minimal risk.
  2. Know the dates as they stand: bans since February 2025, transparency since August 2026, high-risk from December 2027 after the Digital Omnibus. Re-check before launches.
  3. GDPR applies all along: lawful basis, minimisation, where data goes, transparency, automated decisions and DPIAs.
  4. Write it down as you build: register entry, risk notes, eval evidence, guardrails and a plain-words notice. Not legal advice: involve your legal and privacy team.
