Zone 5, Ship

AI UX and trust

How to design AI features people trust the right amount: set honest expectations, make mistakes cheap to fix, and choose how much the AI does on its own. Then redesign an AI screen and test it.

The situation

Ledgerly's AI expense categoriser is accurate 96% of the time. The research team watched ten users: nine clicked "Accept all" in seconds. Two months later, an accountant finds a client's private dinner filed as a business expense.

"The AI was right almost every time!" "Almost. And nobody knew which time it wasn't."

Why predict first? Trying a question before the lesson makes the lesson stick better, even when the first guess is wrong. Rating your confidence trains you to notice what you don't know yet.

Lessons

Three short lessons. Each runs the same way: the idea, the framework, a real example, where it breaks, and a quick drill.

1Calibrated trustThe goal isn't more trust. It's the right amount, matched to what the AI can really do.8 min

The idea

People can trust AI too much or too little. Over-reliance is accepting an AI answer that's wrong. Under-reliance is ignoring an answer that's right. Both cost you: one in mistakes, the other in a feature nobody uses.

Calibrated trust means people rely on the AI when it's likely right, and check it when it's likely wrong. That's a design job, and much of it is the PM's.

The framework

Four levers for calibrating trust:

  1. Say what it can do, with examples, and what it can't. (Microsoft's HAX guideline 1.)
  2. Say how well it does it. Honest precision: a range, not €12,438.17; "usually right for receipts, less so for handwritten ones". (HAX guideline 2.)
  3. Make checking cheap: show the source, highlight what it changed, put the uncertain items first.
  4. Make checking worth it: flag the cases where a mistake costs the most.

The HAX guidelines are 18 guidelines for human-AI interaction from Microsoft researchers (Amershi et al., CHI 2019), grouped by when they apply: at first use, during use, when the AI is wrong, and over time.

Real example

Stanford researchers ran five studies with 731 people on a maze task with AI help. Explanations reduced over-reliance only when checking them was easier than solving the task alone, or when getting it right paid off. When checking was hard, people just accepted the AI's answer, explanation or not.

Source: Vasconcelos et al., "Explanations Can Reduce Overreliance on AI Systems During Decision-Making", CSCW 2023.

Where it breaks

  • Explanations aren't automatically helpful. A long explanation that's hard to check can raise trust without improving decisions.
  • "AI can make mistakes" in small print doesn't calibrate anything. It doesn't tell people when to check.
  • Confidence scores only help if they're accurate and people understand them. Groupings like "please check these 12" often work better than percentages.

2Design for when it's wrongEvery AI feature is wrong sometimes. Great ones make that cheap.9 min

The idea

Classic software is right or broken. AI is right most of the time and wrong some of the time, so the experience of being wrong is part of the product. Ask early: when this is wrong, what does the user see, and what does it cost them?

The framework

The recovery toolkit, from the HAX "when wrong" guidelines and Google's People + AI Guidebook:

PatternWhat it looks like
Easy dismissal (HAX G8)Suggestions in grey; ignoring them costs nothing
Easy correction (HAX G9)Editable fields, one-click "change category", undo
Scope down when in doubt (HAX G10)Ask a clarifying question, or offer options, instead of guessing
Show why (HAX G11)"Filled from studio-nord.de", "based on 3 similar invoices"
Granular feedback (HAX G15)"This is wrong: wrong amount", not just thumbs down
Graceful failure"I couldn't read this receipt. Enter the total?", never a dead end

Real example

Perplexity, the AI answer engine, attaches numbered citations to every answer, linking each claim to its source so people can check it in one click. Its help centre describes this as how the product works, not an extra.

Source: Perplexity Help Center, "How does Perplexity work?"

Where it breaks

  • A citation is not proof. Sources can be wrong, misread or not say what the answer claims. Citations make checking possible; evals (A7) check the citations.
  • Thumbs down with no reason tells you something went wrong, but not what.
  • Undo that expires instantly or hides in a menu isn't really undo.

3Choose the level of autonomySuggest, draft, act with approval, or act alone? Match it to the stakes.9 min

The idea

The same AI capability can ship at very different levels of autonomy. Choosing the level is one of the biggest product decisions in an AI feature, and it's yours to make.

1. Suggest→2. Draft for review→3. Act with approval→4. Act, then report→5. Act silently

The framework

Three questions decide the level:

  1. How bad is a mistake? Money, clients, legal or health: stay low.
  2. Can it be undone? A sent email or a payment can't. A category can.
  3. How accurate is it, measured? Your evals (A7) tell you, not the demo.

Start one level lower than you think, measure, and earn the next level. Make waiting feel short too: stream text as it's written and show progress for long tasks, so people stay in control while the AI works.

Real example

When GitHub launched its Copilot coding agent in May 2025, it gave the agent real autonomy, but inside review: it works on its own branch, pushes commits to a draft pull request with session logs anyone can follow, and its pull requests need human approval before any CI/CD workflows run. The developer who asked the agent for the change can't be the one who approves it.

Source: GitHub, "Meet the new coding agent", May 2025, and The New Stack's launch report.

Where it breaks

  • Approval fatigue. Ask for approval 50 times a day and people click "yes" without reading. Approve in batches, and only where it matters.
  • Silent autonomy hides mistakes until they pile up. Even at level 5, keep a log people can review.
  • One level for everything is rarely right. Low-risk cases can go higher than high-risk ones in the same feature.

Cases

An AI feature designed so that ignoring it costs nothing, and one that put confident AI answers above the world's search results.

Got it right

GitHub Copilot's suggestions

Copilot's code completions appear as grey "ghost text" in the editor: press Tab to accept, keep typing to ignore. Being wrong costs the developer almost nothing. In GitHub's controlled experiment, developers using Copilot finished a coding task 55% faster (1 hour 11 minutes against 2 hours 41 minutes on average) and completed it more often (78% against 70%) (GitHub research blog, 2022). Its later coding agent climbs the autonomy ladder carefully, delivering draft pull requests for human review.

Make the AI's output cheap to ignore and easy to accept, and raise autonomy only inside a review step people already trust.

Got it wrong

Google's AI Overviews

Google rolled out AI Overviews to everyone in the US on 14 May 2024, placing AI answers above search results. Within days, screenshots went viral: it suggested non-toxic glue to keep cheese on pizza, from an old joke forum post, and eating a small rock a day, from a satire site. On 30 May, Google's head of Search, Liz Reid, acknowledged odd and inaccurate answers and listed more than a dozen fixes, including better detection of nonsense queries, less satire and user-generated content, and fewer triggers (Google blog, 30 May 2024; AP).

The position and tone of an AI answer tell people how much to trust it. When it sits on top, sounds sure and has no easy check, every slip becomes a headline.

Show a worked example

Feature: AI expense categories in Ledgerly. Level at launch: draft for review, with bulk accept for confident items.

Expectations: first use shows a sample month already sorted, and says "Usually right for card payments; less sure about cash and transfers". Checking: uncertain items come first, highlighted, each with a one-line reason. Recovery: a category dropdown on every row, Undo for bulk accept, and edits remembered for that payee.

Feedback: "Wrong? Tell us why" with three reasons, feeding the golden set (A7). Next level: auto-file confident items and send a weekly summary, once confident items are 98% correct for eight weeks and edits stay under 2%.

Key takeaways

  1. Aim for calibrated trust. People should rely on the AI when it's likely right and check it when it's likely wrong.
  2. Be honest about how well it works. Show examples, limits and real precision. Put the user's attention where the model is unsure.
  3. Design for being wrong. Easy dismissal, one-click correction, undo, "show why", and specific feedback.
  4. Choose autonomy by stakes. Suggest, draft, approve, act: start one level lower, measure, and earn the next.
