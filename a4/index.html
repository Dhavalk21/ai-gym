Context engineering and RAG

How models answer from your own documents, why they still get it wrong, and how to choose between prompting, retrieval and fine-tuning. Then build RAG twice.

1Embeddings and retrieval, in plain wordsHow a model answers from your documents without being retrained.8 min

The idea

No model was trained on your help centre, your contracts or yesterday's prices. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) fixes that at question time: find the few passages that answer the question, put them in the context, and tell the model to answer from them.

The finding usually uses embeddings: a model turns each passage into a long list of numbers that captures its meaning. Passages about similar things get similar numbers, so "How do I add a second tax rate?" finds the passage about VAT rates even without the same words.

The framework

The RAG relay, four legs, each of which can drop the baton:

1. Documents→2. Chunks→3. Search→4. Generation
  1. Documents: are they right, current and complete?
  2. Chunks: are they split so each piece makes sense alone?
  3. Search: does the right chunk come back for the question?
  4. Generation: does the model use it faithfully, and cite it?

When an answer is wrong, debug the legs in order. Most failures happen before the model writes a word.

Real example

LinkedIn's customer service team combined RAG with a knowledge graph built from past support tickets, so related issues stayed linked instead of being treated as loose text. After about six months in use, median time to resolve an issue fell by 28.6%.

Source: Xu et al., "Retrieval-Augmented Generation with Knowledge Graphs for Customer Service Question Answering", SIGIR 2024.

Where it breaks

  • Your content becomes the AI's quality. Outdated or contradicting articles get retrieved and repeated with confidence.
  • Meaning search misses exact terms. Error codes, IDs and product names often need keyword search too.
  • Retrieved isn't the same as right. The model can still misread or over-generalise a perfect source.

2Chunking, hybrid search and citationsThe details that decide whether the right passage comes back.9 min

The idea

Three choices make or break retrieval:

  • Chunking: split documents along their own structure (sections, paragraphs) so each chunk makes sense alone. Too small loses context; too big buries the answer.
  • Hybrid search: combine meaning-based search (embeddings) with keyword search (often an algorithm called BM25) so both "a second tax rate" and "E-402" work.
  • Reranking: fetch 20 or so candidates, then use a second model to pick the best few.

Then make the answer checkable: number the sources, ask the model to cite them, and show the citations to users.

The framework

A RAG quality checklist:

  1. Each chunk carries its title and section, so "Click Add rate" still says which page it's from.
  2. Search is hybrid, with a reranker if quality matters.
  3. The prompt says: answer only from the sources, cite them, and say when they don't cover the question.
  4. Every answer shows its sources to the user.
  5. Your test set includes questions the documents can't answer.

Real example

In September 2024, Anthropic published Contextual Retrieval: before indexing, a model adds a short note to each chunk explaining where it sits in the document. Combining contextual embeddings with contextual keyword search cut failed retrievals by 49% in its tests, and adding reranking cut them by 67%.

Source: Anthropic, "Introducing Contextual Retrieval", 19 September 2024.

Where it breaks

  • Citations can be decorative. A model can cite a source that doesn't say what it claims. Test that answers match their sources.
  • Thresholds cut both ways. A strict score cut-off avoids junk but drops valid matches, for example questions in another language.
  • Every step adds cost and delay. Reranking and contextual notes are worth it where accuracy matters, not everywhere.

3Prompt, RAG or fine-tune?Three levers, each fixing a different problem. And the bigger idea: context engineering.9 min

The idea

Teams often reach for fine-tuning (training a model further on your own examples) when the real problem is missing knowledge. Each lever fixes something different:

LeverFixesDoesn't fix
PromptingTask, tone, format, rulesKnowledge it never had
RAGPrivate, fresh or changing facts, with sourcesStyle, or reasoning skill
Fine-tuningConsistent style or format at scale, narrow tasks, smaller and cheaper modelsFacts that change; it bakes in a snapshot

The framework

The order of attack: prompt first (cheapest, fastest), add RAG when facts are missing or change, and consider fine-tuning only when a well-prompted, well-grounded model still misses on style, format or cost, and you have hundreds of good examples.

Behind all three sits context engineering: deciding everything the model sees on each call (instructions, retrieved documents, tool results, memory, history), and keeping it small and relevant.

Real example

In September 2025, Anthropic described context engineering as the natural progression of prompt engineering: as agents run over many steps, the job shifts from writing one good prompt to curating what goes into a limited context window at every step, and keeping it as small and high-signal as possible.

Source: Anthropic, "Effective context engineering for AI agents", 2025.

Where it breaks

  • "Just use a long context window" works for a few documents, but costs grow with every call, and Chroma's 2025 tests found reliability drops as input grows.
  • Fine-tuning has upkeep. When the base model updates, you may need to retrain, and its knowledge ages from day one.
  • RAG is a product, not a feature. Someone must own the documents, their freshness and the test set.

Cases

One team that made retrieval respect how its knowledge is really structured, and one launch that retrieved a joke and served it as advice.

Got it right

LinkedIn's support assistant

LinkedIn's customer service team found that treating past support tickets as plain text lost the links between related issues. It built a knowledge graph from the tickets, kept each ticket's structure, and retrieved from that graph. After about six months in use by the team, median time to resolve an issue fell by 28.6% (Xu et al., SIGIR 2024).

Retrieval quality comes from understanding your own content, not from a bigger model.

Got it wrong

Google's AI Overviews

After AI Overviews rolled out across the US in May 2024, one answer told a user to add non-toxic glue to pizza sauce so the cheese would stick. It came from a joke in a Reddit thread from 11 years earlier. Google's head of Search, Liz Reid, wrote that some overviews had taken sarcastic forum content as fact, and Google limited satire, humour and user-generated content in answers.

RAG repeats whatever it retrieves. Decide which sources are trustworthy before you let a model quote them.

Show a worked example

Feature: Ledgerly's help-centre assistant, answering from the 300 public help articles and the last 12 months of release notes.

Freshness: each article has an owner and a review date; superseded versions are archived out of the index, not left beside the new one. Chunking: by section, with the article title and heading added to every chunk.

Search: hybrid (keywords for error codes and menu names, embeddings for meaning), top 20 reranked to 4. Fallback: "I couldn't find that in the help centre", a button to reach support, and the question logged as a content gap for the weekly review.

Key takeaways

  1. RAG answers from your documents at question time. Documents, chunks, search, generation: debug the relay in that order.
  2. Make retrieval robust and answers checkable. Chunk by structure, use hybrid search, cite sources, and allow "not found".
  3. Prompt first, RAG for facts, fine-tune last. Each lever fixes a different problem; context engineering ties them together.
  4. Your content is the AI's quality. Outdated or conflicting documents become confident wrong answers, so give them an owner.
