Zone 1, Understand the machine

How LLMs work, and choosing a model

What a model actually does, why it makes things up, and how to pick the right one for a job. Then see it for yourself in three free tools.

Lessons

Three short lessons. Each runs the same way: the idea, the framework, a real example, where it breaks, and a quick drill.

1Tokens and next-token predictionA model writes one small piece at a time, picking from probabilities.8 min

The idea

A large language model is a function that looks at some text and gives a probability for every possible next piece. It picks one, adds it to the text, and repeats. That loop is all that happens when a chatbot "writes".

The pieces are tokens: whole words, parts of words, digits or punctuation. OpenAI's rule of thumb for English is that one token is about four characters, or three-quarters of a word. Here is an illustrative split:

Please pay invoice INV-2026-0042 by Friday.

Because the model samples from probabilities, the same prompt can give different answers. Temperature controls how adventurous that sampling is: low sticks to the likeliest token, high takes more chances.

The framework

The four dials behind every AI feature:

  1. Tokens in: everything the model reads (instructions, documents, chat history). It has to fit the context window, and you pay for it.
  2. Probabilities: what the model has learned, which you can't change at run time.
  3. Sampling settings: temperature and output length. Low temperature for extraction and classification, higher for brainstorming.
  4. Tokens out: the answer. Output tokens usually cost more than input tokens, and each one adds a little waiting time.

Cost, speed and behaviour all come back to these four.

Real example

Ledgerly serves freelancers across the EU. The same reminder in German usually needs more tokens than in English, and IDs like INV-2026-0042 or a VAT number split into many small tokens. So the same feature can cost more, and fill the context window faster, for some customers than for others. You'll measure this yourself in the lab.

Source: OpenAI Help Center, "What are tokens and how to count them".

Where it breaks

  • Letters and maths: the model sees tokens, not letters, so counting letters or exact arithmetic can trip it up. Use code for exact answers.
  • Temperature 0 isn't a promise: it makes answers far more consistent, but providers don't guarantee identical output every time.
  • A bigger window isn't a better one: in July 2025 Chroma tested 18 models and found answers got less reliable as the input grew, even on simple tasks. Chroma calls this context rot. Send what's relevant, not everything.

2Training, and why models hallucinateWhere a model's knowledge comes from, and why it guesses.9 min

The idea

Models are built in stages. Pre-training on a huge amount of text produces a base model that is very good at continuing documents. Post-training turns it into an assistant: first by fine-tuning on example conversations, then by reinforcement learning, where human or automated feedback rewards better answers. Reasoning models get extra training to work through a problem step by step before they answer.

Post-training matters more than people expect. In OpenAI's 2022 InstructGPT paper (Ouyang et al.), people preferred answers from a 1.3-billion-parameter model tuned with human feedback over the original 175-billion-parameter GPT-3, a model over 100 times larger.

Everything the model "knows" from training is compressed and stops at its training cut-off. It isn't looking anything up.

The framework

Where an answer comes from:

1. Baked inWhat it learned in training. Broad, fuzzy, dated, and impossible to cite.
2. In the contextWhat you put in front of it: your documents, the user's data. Precise and current.
3. From toolsWhat it fetches live: a search, a database, an API. Fresh and checkable.

The rule: for any fact that matters in your product, move it from 1 to 2 or 3.

Real example

In September 2025, OpenAI published research arguing that models hallucinate partly because training and standard evaluations reward guessing over saying "I don't know", just as a multiple-choice test rewards a lucky guess over a blank answer.

Its example from a factual-question test (SimpleQA): an older model, o4-mini, was right 24% of the time and wrong 75% of the time, almost never abstaining. A newer one, gpt-5-thinking-mini, was right 22% of the time but abstained on 52% of questions, so it was wrong only 26% of the time. Similar accuracy, far fewer confident errors.

Source: OpenAI, "Why language models hallucinate", 5 September 2025.

Where it breaks

  • "Don't hallucinate" isn't a fix. Instructions help a little; grounding the answer in sources and checking it helps a lot.
  • Confident tone tells you nothing. A wrong answer reads exactly like a right one.
  • Grounding reduces errors but doesn't end them. A model can still misread or over-generalise from the right document. Show the source so people can check.

3Choosing a modelThere's no best model, only the best one for a job.9 min

The idea

Models come in a few broad kinds, and most products end up using more than one:

  • Frontier models: the most capable, reached through a provider's API. Some are reasoning models that think before answering: better at multi-step problems, slower, and they use more tokens.
  • Small, fast models: much cheaper and quicker, and often good enough for classification, extraction or short drafts.
  • Multimodal models: read images, audio or scanned documents as well as text.
  • Open-weight models: you can download and run them yourself, so data stays on your servers. For example, OpenAI released gpt-oss under the Apache 2.0 licence in August 2025.

The framework

The model job card: answer five questions before you compare a single model.

  1. Quality: what does a good answer look like? Collect 20 to 50 real examples.
  2. Speed: how long can the user wait?
  3. Volume and cost: how often does it run, and what can one run cost?
  4. Input: short text, long documents, images or audio?
  5. Data: where may this data go? (For EU customers, this question comes early.)

Then prototype with a strong model to learn what's possible, and step down to the smallest model that still passes your examples.

Real example

The best-known public ranking is Arena (called LMArena until it rebranded in January 2026), where people compare two anonymous answers and vote. It's useful, and it has limits. In April 2025, researchers led by Cohere published "The Leaderboard Illusion", reporting that a few large providers could test many private versions before release and keep only the best score. In one case, 27 private variants were tested before the Llama 4 release.

Source: Singh et al., "The Leaderboard Illusion", 2025.

Where it breaks

  • Leaderboards measure someone else's tasks. Use them for a shortlist, never for the decision.
  • Model names and prices change every few months. Check the provider's current price list before you decide.
  • Price per token isn't price per task. A reasoning model with a lower token price can still cost more per answer, because it writes many more tokens while thinking.
  • Models get updated and retired. Pin the exact model version, and re-run your examples before you switch.

Drill: Read the brief

Cases

One team that chose its model for the job, and one that trusted fluent text as fact.

Got it right

Cursor's Tab model

Cursor's code editor predicts your next edit as you type, handling over 400 million requests a day by its own count. Rather than call the biggest model available, it runs its own model built for this one job, where speed matters most. In September 2025 Cursor described retraining it with online reinforcement learning, using which suggestions people accepted or rejected. The new model made 21% fewer suggestions with a 28% higher accept rate (Cursor blog, "Improving Cursor Tab with online RL").

Fit the model to the job's speed and volume, and measure quality the way users feel it.

Got it wrong

The ChatGPT court brief

In 2023, lawyers in Mata v. Avianca filed a brief citing court decisions that ChatGPT had made up. When the other side couldn't find the cases, the lawyers stood by them instead of checking. On 22 June 2023, Judge P. Kevin Castel fined the two lawyers and their firm $5,000 and ordered them to send letters to the real judges named as authors of the fake opinions (sanctions order, U.S. District Court, Southern District of New York).

A fluent answer is not a source. Check the facts that matter, and design products that make checking easy.

Key takeaways

  1. A model writes one token at a time, from probabilities. Context, temperature and length shape what you get, and what you pay.
  2. Baked-in knowledge is fuzzy and dated. Put facts that matter in the context or fetch them with tools, and design for confident mistakes.
  3. There's no best model, only the best one for a job. Start from quality, speed, volume, input and data rules.
  4. Leaderboards build a shortlist; your test set makes the decision. Pick the smallest model that passes, and re-test when models change.
