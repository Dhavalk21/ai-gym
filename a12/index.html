Lessons

1The AI spec: evals as acceptance criteriaIn AI products, examples and evals are the spec.10 min

The idea

A classic spec says what the software must do, and QA checks it. An AI feature behaves differently every time, so "it works" needs a new definition: it passes an agreed set of examples, at an agreed rate, within an agreed cost and risk. Those are your acceptance criteria, and writing them is one of the most valuable things a PM does on an AI team.

The framework

The AI spec, section by section (each draws on a station you've done):

SectionWhat it says
Problem and fit (A2)The user, the job, why AI, and what happens today
Examples (A3)15 to 30 real inputs with good outputs, including awkward ones
Context and tools (A4, A6)What the model sees and can do; the simplest pattern that works
Acceptance criteria (A7)Golden set, pass rate per failure type, zero-tolerance failures
Guardrails and UX (A8, A9)Risks and controls, autonomy level, how users check and recover
Cost, latency, compliance (A10, A11)Cost per task budget, p95 latency, risk tier and disclosures
Launch and metrics (lesson 2)Rollout stages, success metric, guardrail metrics, kill switch

Good criteria are specific and measurable: not "accurate answers", but "at least 95% of 200 golden-set questions answered correctly per the help article, zero invented actions, p95 latency under 3 seconds, under €0.002 per answer".

Real example

Anthropic's developer docs start building any LLM application with success criteria, and describe good ones as specific, measurable, achievable and relevant. Their worked example isn't "good performance" but a precise score on a held-out test set, set as a realistic improvement over a baseline. They also stress that most use cases need several criteria at once, such as accuracy, consistency, tone, privacy, latency and price.

Source: Anthropic, "Define success criteria and build evaluations".

Where it breaks

  • Criteria written after the build get set to whatever the system already does. Write them first.
  • One overall score hides the failure that matters. Set a bar per failure type, and zero tolerance where it counts.
  • A spec without examples leaves "good" to each engineer's imagination. Twenty examples beat two pages of adjectives.

2Launching and measuring AI featuresStage the risk, keep a kill switch, and measure outcomes, not activity.9 min

The idea

Evals tell you a feature is ready to meet users. Only users tell you whether it helps them. So AI launches are staged experiments with a safety net, and the metrics need more care than usual, because AI features generate a lot of impressive-looking activity.

The framework

A staged rollout:

Golden set passes→Internal dogfood→5% with holdout→25 to 50%→Everyone

Behind a feature flag, with a kill switch and a written incident plan. Every stage has exit criteria.

A metric set for AI features:

  • Outcome: task success, such as questions resolved without escalation, invoices filed correctly.
  • Quality signals: acceptance or edit rate, thumbs down with reasons, retries, escalations.
  • Adoption: repeat use in week 2 and week 4, not just first tries.
  • Guardrails: zero-tolerance incidents, guardrail triggers, complaints.
  • Economics: cost per task and latency (A10).

Real example

GitHub researchers analysed 2,631 survey responses from Copilot users and matched them with usage data. The best predictor of how productive developers felt wasn't how much code Copilot wrote, or how long its code survived: it was the acceptance rate, the share of shown suggestions that people accepted. A simple quality signal, tied to the user's decision, beat more elaborate metrics.

Source: Ziegler et al., "Productivity Assessment of Neural Code Completion", MAPS 2022.

Where it breaks

  • Activity isn't value. "Messages sent" can rise because people can't get an answer.
  • Novelty fades. First-week ratings are high for most AI features. Watch week-4 retention.
  • Model changes are launches. A new model version goes through the golden set and a canary, like any release.

3AI in your own workUse AI on your own PM work every day, and stay accountable for the result.8 min

The idea

The AI-native PM doesn't only build AI features; they work with AI daily. That makes them faster, and it's the best way to build intuition for what models do well and badly, which feeds straight back into the products they build.

The framework

Where AI helps PMs most:

  • Research synthesis: cluster interview notes and support tickets, then check quotes against the source.
  • First drafts: specs, release notes, stakeholder updates, from your outline and facts.
  • Prototypes: clickable ideas in an hour instead of a week (A5).
  • Data questions: analysis code you run and check, not numbers you take on trust.
  • A critic: "What would a sceptical CFO ask about this plan?"

Four rules: you own the output; verify every fact and number; only put company data into tools your company approves; save prompts that work in a shared library.

Real example

In April 2025, Shopify's CEO Tobi Lütke published an internal memo saying that "reflexive AI usage is now a baseline expectation" at Shopify. Teams asking for more headcount or resources must first show why they can't get what they want done using AI, prototyping should start with AI exploration, and AI use was added to performance and peer reviews.

Source: Tobi Lütke's memo, posted on X on 7 April 2025, as reported by CNBC and Fortune.

Where it breaks

  • Pasting confidential data into unapproved consumer tools is the most common way PMs create a data incident.
  • Polished but wrong: a fluent draft with one invented number does more harm than a rough one.
  • Mandates without practice produce reports of AI use, not better work. Share real workflows and failures.

Cases

A company that used AI on its own engineering work with tight checks, and one that measured an AI launch by cost and volume until quality slipped.

Got it right

Airbnb's AI-powered test migration

Airbnb needed to move nearly 3,500 React test files to a new testing library, estimated at 1.5 years of manual engineering. Its team built a pipeline in which an LLM rewrote each file, then automated checks (tests, linting, type checks) validated it, with retries and richer context for the hard cases; the last files, under a hundred, were fixed by hand. The whole migration took six weeks (Airbnb engineering blog, March 2025).

AI work pays off when "done" is checked automatically: clear acceptance criteria, validation on every output, and people for the long tail.

Got it wrong

Klarna's AI-first customer service

In February 2024, Klarna announced its OpenAI-powered assistant had handled 2.3 million conversations in its first month, two-thirds of its customer service chats, doing the work of 700 full-time agents. By May 2025, its CEO told Bloomberg that cost had been "a too predominant evaluation factor" and that the result was lower quality; Klarna began hiring human agents again and promised customers they could always reach a person (Klarna press release, February 2024; Bloomberg and CX Dive, May 2025).

Volume and cost savings aren't success metrics on their own. Put quality and customer outcomes in the launch criteria, and keep a human route.

Ship it: your AI launch plan

Plan the launch of an AI feature in your own product, and one habit for your own work. Your answers stay in this browser.

Show a worked example

Feature: Ledgerly's AI help assistant. Acceptance criteria: 95% correct on a 200-question golden set (per the help articles), zero invented actions, unnecessary refusals under 5%, p95 latency under 3 seconds, under €0.002 per answer, chatbot disclosure in place.

Stages: one week of internal dogfood, then 5% of users with a 5% holdout for two weeks, then 50%, then everyone; each stage needs the guardrail metrics green. Primary metric: share of questions resolved without escalation or a repeat question within 24 hours, compared with the holdout.

Guardrails: invented actions (zero), thumbs-down rate with reasons, guardrail triggers, cost per answer. Kill switch: any invented action, or thumbs-down above 15% for a day; the PM or on-call engineer flips the flag. My habit: every Monday, summarise last week's flagged traces with AI, then read ten myself.

Key takeaways

  1. Examples and evals are the spec. Write acceptance criteria before you build: golden set, pass rates, zero-tolerance failures, cost and latency.
  2. Stage every launch. Dogfood, small percentage with a holdout, then wider, behind a flag with a kill switch. Model changes are launches too.
  3. Measure outcomes, not activity. Task success, quality signals, retention, guardrails and cost per task.
  4. Work with AI every day, with approved tools, verified facts and a shared prompt library. You own the output.
